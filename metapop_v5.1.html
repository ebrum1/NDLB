No Dog Left Behind – which dogs, and at what cost. A country is many districts, and the country is free only when every district reaches zero at the same time. Inside each district, what counts is whether the vaccine reaches the dogs that carry transmission, the dogs on the street, not how many dogs are counted.

What is new in v5.1. The country map switches between reported coverage, which is what a campaign report shows, and effective immunity, which is what the dogs have just after a settled round. The two agree only when the vaccine works and the street dogs are reached. Vaccine efficacy now runs from 0%, a placebo that is delivered, paid for and reported like any vaccine, to 100%.

What v5 added. v5 keeps v4's engine and asks one more question: which dogs do the doses reach, and at what cost? Each district now holds six kinds of dog, built in from the advanced model's Sri Lanka dog population and grouped here into the A-team tiers. Each channel reaches some kinds of dog and not others, and it reaches the same dogs every round. Door-to-door reaches household dogs and many owned roaming dogs; a street sweep meets the roaming dogs and catches most of them; oral bait reaches many of the wary dogs that teams struggle to catch. Coverage is now an output of the programme you choose, not a number you type in. R0 defaults to 1.8, a deliberately pessimistic planning value.

Why street dogs count most. A dog's weight in transmission rises with the square of how much it roams. We assume proportionate mixing: a rabid dog's bites fall on household and street dogs in proportion to how active each group is, so about two-thirds land on street dogs. Under that assumption a district's R is exactly R0 × (1 − effective immunity), where effective immunity weights each vaccinated dog by its share of transmission. The 44% of dogs on the street carry 80% of transmission, and a confined dog counts for a four-hundredth of an unowned one. Coverage counts every dog the same, so a programme can report high coverage and still leave the street dogs, and R, almost untouched.

Sweeping productivity. Sweeping effort is fixed at 3.1 team-days per 1,000 dogs a round, the sweeping establishment in the r18 Sri Lanka budget as it stands in the Sri Lanka room (67% door-to-door, 33% sweeping: 7,838 team-days a round for 2.5 million dogs). The lever is how many dogs a team vaccinates in a day at the start of a sweep. Teams slow down as the easy dogs are done and the wary ones are left, and with collars they stop when every dog they can meet wears one. Without collars a team cannot tell a vaccinated dog from an unvaccinated one, so part of its effort and its vaccine goes on dogs already done: dogs vaccinated at the door, and dogs the sweep itself has already caught. Bait teams without collars likewise offer baits to dogs that are already vaccinated. Because effort is fixed, dropping collars can push a programme close to the threshold, and the cost penalty is then large; where more effort could be bought to compensate, it would be smaller.

Costs. Door-to-door and sweeping unit costs come from the r18 Sri Lanka budget as it stands in the Sri Lanka room (17 September 2026 export), recurring items at year-1 prices, with the biohazard box corrected to LKR 1,000 per box of 300 doses. Door-to-door delivery is an average at the budget's volume and includes the VS coordinators. The room has no oral bait, so the bait cost (LKR 2,500 a bait, unsourced, plus wastage and distribution) comes from the 16 September version. Costs cover delivery only: vaccine, collars, door-to-door and sweeping teams, and baits. Training, surveillance, programme management beyond the coordinators, and sterilisation stay in the budget calculator. Each run pays for every round it needs up to the year it eliminates; runs that never eliminate pay for every round inside the 12-year horizon. Exchange rates: US$1 = LKR 330.76 = £0.7555, 15 October 2026.

How it works. Campaigns lift an immune saw-tooth for each kind of dog in each district: annual by default, spread over the district campaign length and rolled out across districts over the overall campaign length. A district's R follows the effective immunity, and the infection is a stochastic branching process stepped once per ≈7-week rabies generation. Immunity falls between rounds as dogs die and are replaced and protection wanes, so it is lower just before the next round. A few weeks below the threshold does little harm if the next round arrives on time; immunity that stays below it lets rabies re-establish. Districts are coupled on a nearest-neighbour grid; the coupled share of a district's infectious pressure moves to its neighbours and reproduces at the receiving district's own R, so a well-vaccinated neighbour resists re-seeding. The country counts as eliminated only when every district reaches zero in the same generation. The elimination probability carries its Monte Carlo interval. Protection wanes at a constant rate, and every result comes from 200 model runs with a fixed random seed, so the same settings always give the same answer.

What v5 does not claim.

  • It is a teaching tool, not a forecast. Compare programmes; do not read the levels as predictions for any country.
  • The six kinds of dog, their contact weights and every access figure are judgement taken from the advanced model, not field measurements. They describe Sri Lanka, and every district uses the same mix.
  • Proportionate mixing between kinds of dog is assumed. If street dogs mainly bite street dogs, household dogs matter even less; the lesson becomes stronger, it does not reverse.
  • The access figures are behavioural quantities: owners presenting and restraining their dogs, communities tolerating or feeding street dogs. Round 1 field data should replace them.
  • A dog that no channel can reach is missed every round; a dog within a channel's reach is missed at random.
  • Vaccine failure is random: each dose fails on its own, so a dog whose dose failed gets a fresh chance at the next round. That is the most forgiving reading. Dogs that never respond, a weak vaccine that also protects for less time, or a broken cold chain that fails a whole district every round would all do much more harm.
  • There is no space inside a district: no dispersal kernel and no local depletion, so the advanced model's finding that a dense urban block can sustain rabies on its own cannot appear here.
  • Finding a dog takes no time: a sweeping team spends its whole day on catch attempts, so with high enough productivity it eventually catches even the wariest dogs. In reality the last dogs are also the hardest to find.
  • Puppies do not grow up: a vaccinated puppy stays in the puppy group rather than carrying its immunity into the adult groups, and there is no puppy cohort carry-over.
  • There is no emergency response, no imported cases, no verification of freedom, and no check on team logistics or campaign windows.
  • v4's limits still apply: a regular grid with neighbour-only coupling and no human-mediated dog movement; a ceiling of 300 new cases per district per generation; an initial burden fixed per district rather than scaled to its size; no critical community size; one deterministic immunity backbone shared by all runs; no observation layer, so observed zero and true zero cannot be told apart; elimination that, once reached, cannot be lost; and map colour bands fixed at 80, 60 and 45%.
